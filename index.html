01 / PROBLEM

Several reports, no shared view

Sales performance and stock health were interpreted in isolation. The design challenge was to connect demand, margin, availability, and purchasing signals without overwhelming the user.

02 / APPROACH

One model, three decision paths

A star schema joins transactions, products, stores, suppliers, and dates. Measures are grouped around executive health, category diagnosis, and replenishment action.

Start with the decision, then design the dashboard

A dashboard should reduce the distance between a question and an action. Before choosing a chart, write down the decision, its owner, its frequency, and the evidence needed to make it.

That short brief becomes a useful filter. A metric stays only if it changes interpretation or action. Navigation follows the natural sequence: status, explanation, then detail. The result is usually smaller than the initial request—and much easier to use.

Five checks every recurring report should expose

Trust improves when the report shows its own health. Display when the data was refreshed, whether expected records arrived, whether totals reconcile, which exceptions remain, and who owns the source.

These checks do not replace data-quality work. They make its current state visible, allowing users to distinguish a business change from a pipeline problem.

Automate the boring part, document the risky part

Good automation removes repetition while preserving control. Inputs should be validated, failures should be explicit, and every output should be traceable to a source and run.

The operating note matters as much as the script: what it expects, how it fails, who receives the result, and how a person can recover. That is what turns a shortcut into a dependable process.
